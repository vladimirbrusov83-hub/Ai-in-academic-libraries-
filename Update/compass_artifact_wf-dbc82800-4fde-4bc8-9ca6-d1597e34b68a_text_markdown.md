# The State of AI in Academic Libraries, 2025–2026: A Research Brief for AI Literacy Curriculum Design

## TL;DR
- **The profession now has its first purpose-built standard.** ACRL's "AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers" (approved October 3, 2025) is the new center of gravity, but it is deliberately a flexible framework — not a curriculum — and it explicitly preserves a "right to refuse" AI, leaving working librarians without concrete, role-specific, hands-on training that any AI literacy curriculum must now supply.
- **Adoption is real but shallow and uneven.** Two-thirds of libraries are exploring or implementing AI (Clarivate Pulse 2025: 67%, up from 63% in 2024, with 33% now in implementation stages — three times as many as in 2024), and a nationwide survey found 66.5% of librarians use generative AI at work — but most use is individual experimentation with general LLMs (ChatGPT) for drafting and summarizing, U.S. optimism is the lowest in the world (7%), budget has overtaken skills as the top barrier (62% overall; 66% of academic libraries), and 70.03% of librarians reported feeling unprepared to adopt GenAI (Lo, 2024).
- **The frontier is shifting from "assistance" to "action."** Vendor tools (Ex Libris Primo Research Assistant, OCLC's December 2025 AI cataloging features, ProQuest Research Assistant) are now in production, agentic AI is entering the discourse and the ACRL competencies, and the sharpest unmet curriculum needs are: hands-on tool/prompt skills, vendor-AI and data-privacy evaluation, agentic-AI governance, and critical/"refusal" perspectives — with community-college and instruction-focused contexts especially underserved.

## Key Findings

**1. Frameworks & Standards**
- ACRL's **"AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers"** was approved by the ACRL Board on **October 3, 2025**, co-chaired by Keven Jeffery and Jason Coleman. It is organized into **five guiding mindsets** (curiosity, skepticism, judgment, responsibility, collaboration) and **four competency categories** (Ethical Considerations; Knowledge & Understanding; Analysis & Evaluation; Use & Application), structured after Ng et al. (2021) and Hervieux & Wheatley (2024).
- The framework is intentionally **product-agnostic and adaptable**, meant as "a guiding framework for the creation of training programs," not a curriculum. It explicitly names **agentic AI** and **RAG** in its Knowledge & Understanding competency.
- It takes a notable **"right to refuse" stance**: "adoption of AI technologies is neither necessary nor beneficial in all cases" (p. 8) — a refusal-preserving move praised by commentators as unusual among competency documents.
- Critiques/alternatives: critical-librarianship scholarship pushes back on the "feeling rules" of AI adoption (Lead Pipe, 2025), Library Trends 73(4) includes "Against AI: Critical Refusal in the Library," and a "Critical AI Literacy Framework"/RACBAC standard (International Journal of Librarianship, 2025) proposes evaluation-focused alternatives.
- International: **IFLA** published an "Entry Point to Libraries and AI" (June 2025) and a major IFLA Journal AI special issue (Oct 2025); the older IFLA FAIFE Statement remains in force. **ARL's** seven "Research Libraries Guiding Principles for AI" (April 2024) remain foundational ("no human, no AI"). **CILIP** (UK) released "AI and the UK Library Profession: Survey Report 2025" (authored by Dr. Andrew Cox, University of Sheffield).
- **Newer/additional statements to watch:** the **Library Copyright Alliance "Statement on Copyright and Generative Artificial Intelligence"** (v4.0, October 2025) asserting existing copyright law is adequate; **ARL & CNI "Strategic Implications of AI Futures for Research Libraries"** (March 2026); and a **DRAFT ALA "Guidance on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Libraries"** (released April 2026, comment period closed May 8, 2026, slated for Council consideration at the June 2026 ALA Annual — *not yet adopted; treat as draft*).

**2. Practitioner Adoption**
- **Clarivate "Pulse of the Library 2025"** (released Oct 30, 2025; 2,032 respondents, 109 countries, 77% academic; DOI 10.14322/pulse.of.the.library.2025): 67% exploring/implementing AI (up from 63% in 2024), with 33% now in implementation stages — three times as many as in 2024; academic libraries more likely to be in initial implementation (21%) or moderate deployment (13%); only 28% of academic libraries have no AI plans (vs. 54% of public). Budget overtook skills as the top barrier (62% overall; 66% for academic libraries); U.S. optimism lowest globally (7%, vs. 27–31% in Asia/China/rest of world).
- **Lili Luo's nationwide survey** (SJSU, published Nov 15, 2025; 272 librarians): 66.5% use generative AI at work; text tools (ChatGPT) dominant; effectiveness rated 6.76/10; top challenges were accuracy/reliability; over half wanted hands-on training.
- **Leo Lo's earlier baseline** ("Evaluating AI Literacy in Academic Libraries," *College & Research Libraries* 85(5), 2024; n=760): 70.03% of respondents reported feeling unprepared to adopt GenAI tools within the coming year.
- Highest-adoption workflows: drafting/communications, summarization, reference support, instruction prep, and metadata/cataloging. Top stated objectives: support for student learning and content discovery.
- Barriers: budget, skills/expertise gaps, ethics/integrity concerns, lack of clear institutional policy, and tool access (premium tiers).

**3. Curriculum & Training Gaps**
- Existing offerings: ACRL/Choice + Clarivate **"Generative AI Literacy Essentials for Academic Librarians"** (free, 8-module micro-course launched July 2025, mapped to the ACRL framework); Library Journal courses ("Foundations of Integrating AI in Libraries," 2025); ALA Core webinars; Library 2.0 "AI Deep Dive" workshops; WebJunction; iSchool offerings.
- iSchools are building AI specializations primarily for **degree students**, not current practitioners — e.g., University of Washington's MSIM AI specialization (low-/no-code emphasis, online from spring 2026) and Illinois's Library Trends two-part AI series.
- **"Vibe coding"** (term coined by Andrej Karpathy, Feb 2025; Collins Word of the Year 2025) and no-code/low-code app building are appearing in iSchool curricula (UW IMT 523 explicitly teaches low-/no-code AI tools) but are **not yet broadly present in practitioner-facing library training** — a clear gap.
- Most-requested but underserved: hands-on practice, prompt engineering, tool evaluation, and ethics applied to real workflows.

**4. Tool Landscape**
- Library-specific tools in production: **Ex Libris Primo Research Assistant** (GA to all Primo customers; RAG over the ~5-billion-record Central Discovery Index) and **Summon Research Assistant**; **AI Metadata Assistant for Alma**; **Specto** for digital collections; all on the Clarivate Academic AI platform.
- **OCLC** added AI cataloging features to WorldShare Record Manager and Connexion (announced **December 8, 2025**) suggesting DDC/LCC/LCSH; pilot catalogers "reported saving up to 20 minutes per title" and described the tools as a "safety net." OCLC also ran an AI WorldCat de-duplication test (Feb 11, 2025, targeting 500,000 record pairs of print English books) and updated BFAS §3.5 for recording AI-generated metadata.
- **ProQuest Research Assistant** (integrated into ProQuest Central, Feb 2025) and many database-level assistants (JSTOR, EBSCO AI Insights, Ebook Central, Scopus). **Springshare LibAnswers** chatbot remains rule-based.
- General LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) appear to be **complementing rather than displacing** library-specific tools — they dominate day-to-day individual use while vendor tools handle discovery/metadata at scale.

**5. Community College & Instruction-Focused Contexts**
- Community college students comprise **39% of all U.S. undergraduates** (AACC Fast Facts 2025), with nearly half (49%) of Hispanic college students and 32% first-generation learners — populations most exposed to AI-driven labor disruption, making equitable AI literacy urgent.
- California Community Colleges launched an **AI Fellows program** (Sept 2025; 13 fellows, 116 colleges, 2.1M students). Community-college framing centers workforce relevance, industry alignment, and access over research-tool sophistication.
- Equity of access is the defining challenge: "freemium" tiers create divides; calls for ARL/ACRL consortial purchasing. Community-college libraries (e.g., Salt Lake Community College's GenAI LibGuide) emphasize practical, responsible-use instruction; critical AI literacy resists "catch-up" deficit narratives.
- Data specifically comparing community-college vs. R1 staff attitudes is sparse — a flagged gap.

**6. Agentic AI & Automation**
- Discourse is accelerating: the ACRL competencies define agentic AI as "autonomous systems that set goals, plan tasks, and act with minimal guidance" and ask workers to "track how this autonomy could reshape library workflows."
- Real, library-adjacent implementations are mostly vendor-driven and research-facing (Elsevier's Scopus-grounded agentic "Deep Research"); broad multi-step automation inside library operations remains early. The ARL/CNI 2026 report notes early agentic workflow automation is "moving AI from 'assistance' to 'action,' raising immediate governance requirements for authorization, auditability, containment, and quality control."
- What's being automated: metadata suggestion, de-duplication, discovery summarization, routine drafting. What librarians protect as human work: judgment, ethics, instruction, relationship-based reference (ARL's "no human, no AI").

**7. Ethical & Policy Landscape**
- Active debates: equitable access, environmental impact, labor/displacement, AI detection unreliability, bias, vendor "black-box" algorithms, and patron-data privacy.
- Librarians are positioning as **policy leaders** but often arrive late: scholarship (Journal of Library Administration, 2025) documents policy gaps and calls for librarians to lead institutional AI policy. A reported gap: ~70% of librarians felt unprepared (Lo, 2024) while students adopt rapidly (Elsevier/Sage "Librarian Futures Part 4" reported 28% of students use AI daily or every couple of days, plus another 28% weekly).
- The draft ALA guidance (April 2026) directly addresses vendor AI and privacy: "Libraries will not adopt AI tools that fail to protect individuals' privacy, personal data, and security," and "Libraries will require AI vendors to provide algorithmic transparency."
- Notable statements: ACRL competencies (Oct 2025); ARL principles (2024); IFLA Entry Point (2025); Library Copyright Alliance copyright/AI statements (Oct 2025).

## Details

### 1. Frameworks & Standards
The single most important development since October 2025 is ACRL's **"AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers,"** approved by the ACRL Board on October 3, 2025 and freely posted in ACRL's Standards, Guidelines, and Frameworks section. The task force (co-chairs Keven Jeffery and Jason Coleman; members including Leo S. Lo as board liaison, Nicole Hennig, Michael Paulus, and others) built it on Leo Lo's 2025 "AI Literacy: A Guide for Academic Libraries" and the conceptual structure of Ng et al. (2021). It defines AI literacy as the ability to "understand, use, and think critically about AI technologies and their impact on society, ethics, and everyday life," organizing guidance into five mindsets and four competency categories.

Two features matter most for curriculum design. First, it is deliberately **non-prescriptive**: "because AI technologies are developing rapidly … it does not reference specific products, models, or job functions," and it is meant as a foundation for training programs that institutions must build themselves. Second, it embeds a **refusal-preserving stance** — AI literacy "should not imply an obligation to indiscriminately adopt AI tools." Commentators highlighted this as distinctive among competency frameworks. The Knowledge & Understanding category explicitly requires understanding RAG and agentic AI, plus the caution that AI-detection tools "are not completely accurate and can be circumvented."

Critiques and alternatives are forming a genuine counter-discourse. The Lead Pipe essay "Investigating the 'Feeling Rules' of Generative AI" (2025) argues much AI-literacy rhetoric "mandate[s] GenAI adoption" and crowds out skepticism; Library Trends 73(4) (Illinois iSchool, 2025) includes "Against AI: Critical Refusal in the Library." A database-anchored "Critical AI Literacy Framework"/RACBAC standard (Relevance, Accuracy, Coverage, Bias, Authority, Currency; International Journal of Librarianship, 2025) offers an evaluation-first alternative.

Internationally, IFLA released its "Entry Point to Libraries and AI" (June 2025) and devoted IFLA Journal 51(3) (Oct 2025) to AI; ARL's seven guiding principles (April 2024) remain the North American values anchor; CILIP (UK) published its AI survey report in 2025. Newer documents include the Library Copyright Alliance's October 2025 copyright/GenAI statement (holding that existing copyright law is adequate), the March 2026 ARL/CNI futures report, and ALA's April 2026 draft AI guidance (not yet adopted).

### 2. Practitioner Adoption
The **Clarivate "Pulse of the Library 2025"** report (released October 30, 2025; survey fielded June–July 2025; 2,032 respondents across 109 countries; 77% academic; analysis with TBI Communications) is the key dataset. AI exploration/implementation rose to 67% (from 63% in 2024), with 33% of libraries now in implementation stages — three times the 2024 share. Academic libraries are progressing faster than public ones (21% initial implementation, 13% moderate deployment; only 28% with no plans vs. 54% of public libraries). Crucially, **budget overtook skills as the top barrier** ("Budgets now represent the greatest concern for AI adoption (62%), up from 56% in 2024"; 66% among academic libraries specifically), and U.S./U.K. librarians report the lowest optimism worldwide (7%), versus 27–31% in Asia and the rest of the world. Libraries with AI literacy in formal onboarding (28%), dedicated time/resources (23.3%), and managerial encouragement (24.2%) implement more and report higher confidence.

**Lili Luo's** "Use of Generative AI in Aiding Daily Professional Tasks" (SJSU, published Nov 15, 2025; 272 librarians) found 66.5% use GenAI at work, overwhelmingly text tools like ChatGPT for drafting reports/presentations, teaching examples, and reference support; mean effectiveness 6.76/10; leading concerns were inconsistent accuracy/reliability; over half wanted hands-on training, and the most common reason non-users gave was "lack of interest" — a cultural, not just technical, barrier. Luo points to "AI sandboxes" (Harvard's model) as institutional supports. The earlier baseline is Lo's 2024 *College & Research Libraries* survey (n=760), in which "70.03% of respondents (those who 'strongly disagree' or 'disagree') admit a lack of preparedness" to adopt GenAI within the coming year.

### 3. Curriculum & Training Gaps
The flagship practitioner offering is the free ACRL/Choice + Clarivate micro-course **"Generative AI Literacy Essentials for Academic Librarians"** (8 modules, launched mid-July 2025, mapped to the ACRL AI literacy framework, written by academic library workers). Library Journal ran fee-based cohort courses in 2025 ("Foundations of Integrating AI in Libraries," March 2025; "Empowering Library Users with AI," May 2025), ALA Core webinars, Library 2.0 "AI Deep Dive" workshops (Reed Hepler), and WebJunction sessions round out the landscape.

iSchools are investing heavily but mostly for **degree-seeking students**: the University of Washington MSIM **AI specialization** (IMT 523 teaches low-/no-code GenAI implementation; available online from spring 2026) and Illinois's two-part Library Trends AI series and ALISE "Are we ready? Generative AI and the LIS curriculum." For current practitioners, formal iSchool offerings are thinner — continuing-education and association webinars carry most of the load.

**"Vibe coding"/no-code building**: the concept (Karpathy, Feb 2025; Collins Word of the Year 2025; named in tools like Replit, Lovable, Bolt, Cursor) is reaching iSchool curricula (UW explicitly teaches low-/no-code AI tools) but is largely absent from practitioner-facing library training — an opportunity, given librarians' appetite for building custom GPTs/chatbots (the Georgia Southern conference paper on building a custom library virtual assistant via OpenAI's GPT Builder is an early example).

### 4. Tool Landscape
Library-specific AI is now in production. **Ex Libris/Clarivate**: Primo Research Assistant (GA to all Primo customers; RAG over the ~5-billion-record Central Discovery Index, surfacing five most-relevant sources with citations; deployed e.g. at University of Florida), Summon Research Assistant, AI Metadata Assistant for Alma, and Specto for digital collections, all on the Clarivate Academic AI platform. **OCLC** announced (Dec 8, 2025) AI cataloging assistance in WorldShare Record Manager and Connexion that suggests DDC/LCC numbers and LCSH from WorldCat data, with catalogers retaining accept/reject control and pilot users reporting savings of "up to 20 minutes per title"; OCLC also ran an AI WorldCat de-duplication test (Feb 11, 2025, 500,000 record pairs) and updated Bibliographic Formats and Standards §3.5 to record AI-generated metadata provenance. **ProQuest Research Assistant** integrated into ProQuest Central (Feb 2025); database-level assistants proliferate (JSTOR, EBSCO AI Insights, Ebook Central, Scopus). Springshare's LibAnswers chatbot remains rule-based.

General-purpose LLMs complement rather than displace these: librarians reach for ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini/Copilot for daily drafting and ideation, while vendor tools handle discovery and metadata at institutional scale. For instruction itself, librarians use ChatGPT-generated essays as critical-evaluation exercises (the widely cited library-instruction case study) and tools like Perplexity, Semantic Scholar, and Research Rabbit in research-skills teaching.

### 5. Community College & Instruction-Focused Contexts
Community college students comprise 39% of U.S. undergraduates (AACC Fast Facts 2025), 32% are first-generation, and most attend part-time while working. These learners are simultaneously the most exposed to AI labor disruption and the least likely to have institutional AI access — making equity the defining issue. California's community-college system launched an AI Fellows program (Sept 2025, 13 fellows for 116 colleges/2.1M students), and a Harvard Business School convening (Nov 2025) underscored thin faculty-training pipelines and unbuilt credentialing.

Community-college library AI work centers practical, responsible-use instruction (Salt Lake Community College's GenAI LibGuide; California Conference on Library Instruction sessions on critical AI literacy that resist the "you must catch up to university peers" deficit narrative). Compared with R1 research libraries — where the emphasis is research-data support, scholarly-communication, and sophisticated discovery tools — community-college contexts prioritize workforce alignment, transfer pathways, and first-contact information literacy. Hard comparative data on adoption rates and attitudes between the two staff populations is sparse and represents a genuine evidence gap.

### 6. Agentic AI & Automation
The ACRL competencies formalize agentic AI as an expected concept. In practice, agentic features are appearing first in vendor research tools (Elsevier's Scopus-grounded "Deep Research" that shows its multi-step reasoning on screen) rather than in core library operations. The ARL/CNI March 2026 report frames early agentic workflow automation as moving AI from "assistance" to "action," with governance (authorization, auditability, containment, quality control) as the central concern. Cross-sector 2026 discourse echoes this. Within libraries, automation targets routine, verifiable tasks — metadata suggestion, de-duplication, discovery summarization, drafting — while librarians explicitly protect judgment, ethics, instruction, and relationship-based reference (ARL's "no human, no AI").

### 7. Ethical & Policy Landscape
The live debates are equitable access, environmental cost, labor and deskilling, the unreliability of AI detectors, bias, vendor black-box opacity, and patron-data privacy. Librarians are increasingly framed as the natural campus leaders on AI policy, yet scholarship (Journal of Library Administration, 2025) documents that many institutional AI policies have gaps and that libraries are under-represented in writing them. The readiness gap is stark: 70.03% of librarians reported being unprepared to adopt GenAI (Lo, 2024) even as student adoption surges (Elsevier/Sage "Librarian Futures Part 4," 2025: 28% of students use AI daily/near-daily, plus 28% weekly; 58% said they would feel confident a library-approved tool aligns with their institution's AI policy). OCLC's metadata-workflow working group (April–June 2025) surfaced practitioner concerns about AI environmental impact and the lifecycle/provenance of AI-generated records. The Library Copyright Alliance continues to lead on copyright-and-AI positions (Oct 2025 statement), and ALA's April 2026 draft guidance would require vendor algorithmic transparency and bar tools that fail to protect patron privacy.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Foundations (immediate, weeks 1–4).** Anchor any curriculum in the **ACRL AI Competencies (Oct 2025)**, but translate its four categories into concrete, role-specific modules the framework deliberately leaves open. Include foundational concepts (LLMs, RAG, agentic AI, hallucination, detection unreliability) and the "right to refuse" framing so the curriculum is not perceived as adoption advocacy. *Benchmark to advance:* learners can define RAG/agentic AI and articulate when NOT to use AI.

**Stage 2 — Hands-on practice (weeks 5–10).** Directly address the most-requested, most-underserved need: supervised, hands-on time with both general LLMs and library-specific tools (Primo/Summon Research Assistant, OCLC's new cataloging AI, ProQuest Research Assistant), plus prompt engineering. Add an introductory **no-code/"vibe coding" module** (custom GPTs, simple chatbots) — a gap in current offerings and a high-leverage skill. *Benchmark:* each learner ships one small AI artifact (a custom GPT, a prompt library, or an AI-literacy lesson plan).

**Stage 3 — Evaluation, vendor AI & governance (weeks 11–14).** Teach vendor-AI due diligence: data privacy, algorithmic transparency, black-box risks, environmental cost, and contract/licensing questions. Use the IFLA "Entry Point" scenarios, OCLC metadata-provenance practices, and the ALA draft guidance's vendor-transparency language as cases. Cover agentic-AI governance (human-in-the-loop, auditability) per the ARL/CNI framing. *Benchmark:* learners can complete a vendor-AI evaluation checklist for one tool their library is considering.

**Stage 4 — Instruction & policy leadership (weeks 15–16, ongoing).** Equip librarians to teach critical AI literacy (ChatGPT-essay evaluation exercises, RACBAC-style source evaluation) and to co-author institutional/student AI policies — closing the documented gap in which libraries are under-represented in policy-writing. *Benchmark:* learners draft or revise one classroom activity and one policy contribution.

**Differentiation for community-college and instruction-focused librarians.** Build a parallel track emphasizing (a) equity of access and free-tier limitations; (b) first-generation/workforce-learner needs and resisting deficit narratives; (c) practical, transferable information-literacy framing over research-data sophistication; and (d) low-cost, no-license tooling. Advocate at the association level for consortial AI purchasing so community-college libraries are not priced out.

**Thresholds that would change these recommendations.** If a future ACRL/Clarivate survey shows U.S. optimism and preparedness rising sharply (e.g., the 70% unprepared figure falling below ~40%), shift emphasis from foundational confidence-building toward advanced governance and agentic-workflow design. If agentic features become standard in ILS/discovery layers, elevate Stage 3 governance content to the core. If the ALA AI guidance is adopted at the June 2026 Annual, integrate it as a co-anchor alongside the ACRL competencies.

## Caveats
- **Recency/uncertainty flags:** Several items are vendor-published (Clarivate/Ex Libris, OCLC, Elsevier, ProQuest) and carry promotional framing; adoption claims and time-savings ("up to 20 minutes per title") are vendor- or pilot-reported, not independently audited.
- **Draft-status flag:** ALA's "Guidance on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Libraries" was a **draft** as of the research date (comment period closed May 8, 2026; final Council action expected at the June 2026 ALA Annual). Do not cite as adopted ALA policy until confirmed.
- **Forward-looking language:** Much agentic-AI material is predictive (enterprise "2026 trend" pieces use "will"/"could"); real library agentic implementations remain early and largely research-facing.
- **Evidence gaps:** Direct, quantified comparisons of community-college vs. R1 library staff AI adoption and attitudes are sparse; the report infers differences from context. Practitioner-facing "vibe coding" training is nearly absent, so its presence in LIS education is asserted mainly from iSchool degree curricula.
- The 70.03% "unprepared" figure derives from Lo's 2024 survey (n=760) and may be dated given rapid change; treat as a 2024 baseline, not a 2026 measurement.
- This brief prioritizes 2025–2026 sources; where only 2024 anchors exist (ARL principles, Lo survey) that is noted.
